# Porch Caricature

Irreverent local-culture field guides with a practical social map underneath the jokes.

> **Vero Beach: where everybody wants privacy, everybody knows everybody, and somehow everybody knows your damn business.**

## What this is

A **Porch Caricature** is a memorable, exaggerated sketch of a place and its social habits. The joke earns attention; the map underneath identifies what people value, who connects whom, where trust forms, and how to participate without acting like a walking estimate form.

```text
local curiosity + irreverent observation + genuine respect
→ a better conversation
→ a remembered follow-up
→ a trusted introduction
→ a durable local relationship
```

This is a working hypothesis, not demographic truth. Roast the local ritual, status performance, or development pattern—not protected traits or real hardship. If the joke makes someone feel seen, keep talking. If it makes them feel reduced, congratulations: you built a wall during a relationship exercise.

## Vero Beach field guide

### The central caricature

Vero Beach is where people move to escape the rat race, then spend three hours at a charity luncheon explaining how important they used to be.

The useful truth underneath: Vero often rewards discretion, familiarity, community participation, craftsmanship, and trusted introductions more than loud self-promotion. Remember names, keep promises, respect local history, and show up twice.

### Local language

The middle column is a comic listening prompt, not a literal translation.

| What people say | What it may be testing | Useful response |
|---|---|---|
| “Are you on the island?” | Geography, price tier, or social location | “What part of town feels like home to you?” |
| “Are you here year-round?” | Commitment versus seasonal presence | “How has the seasonal rhythm affected your projects?” |
| “We've been here 30 years.” | Local memory and earned standing | “What change mattered most?” |
| “We like to keep Vero small.” | Growth anxiety and local character | “What do you most want Vero to keep as it grows?” |
| “Which club do you belong to?” | Social placement and mutual connections | Answer plainly; never cosplay membership. Ask what institution or cause they value. |
| “We're very involved in the community.” | Service, status, or both | “Which work has felt most worthwhile?” |
| “I know the family.” | Multi-generation context and reputation | “Then I assume you know the version that never makes the brochure.” |
| “We should have lunch sometime.” | Invitation or polished exit hatch | Follow up once with a specific invitation. If it floats away, let it die with dignity. |
| “It's not like South Florida.” | Identity through contrast | “What difference do you notice first?” |
| “I'm semi-retired.” | Still active, selectively | “What kind of work still gets you interested?” |
| “Who did your work?” | Reputation check and referral opening | Give the name, scope, and why it worked—no vague superlatives. |
| “Can you recommend somebody?” | A trust transfer | Refer only people you would trust with your own reputation; close the loop. |

## The five-layer social map

### 1. Geography

Map where people live, work, worship, serve, spend leisure time, and own property. Go beyond island versus mainland: include seasonal versus year-round life, old neighborhoods versus new growth, school and church networks, jobsite corridors, and repeated paths.

Track local names and boundaries; housing and commercial patterns; seasonal rhythms; institutions and events; construction concerns such as salt, storms, permitting, drainage, preservation, and growth; and who can correct the assumptions.

**Rule:** an address opens a question. It does not reveal budget, politics, or influence.

### 2. Social groups

Working groups may include:

- Longtime local families and Old Florida lifers
- Barrier-island owners and seasonal residents
- Contractors, suppliers, property managers, architects, designers, engineers, and real estate professionals
- Small-business owners and mainland operators
- Arts, education, preservation, and nonprofit participants
- Church communities, school families, youth sports, and neighborhood associations
- Younger professionals, entrepreneurs, and recent arrivals
- Hospitality, marine, healthcare, government, and civic workers
- Homeowners linked by the same building, storm, insurance, or neighborhood problem

For each group, record what earns respect, what wastes time, how introductions happen, and what evidence earns confidence. Useful connectors usually belong to several groups.

### 3. Influence

Separate title from actual influence:

1. **Formal:** officials, institutional leaders, board members, executives, donors, licensed professionals.
2. **Operational:** people who can answer the permit question, source material, rescue a schedule, or kill a bad idea early.
3. **Reputational:** longtime residents, respected tradespeople, clergy, educators, organizers, and repeat connectors.
4. **Relational:** people who can put the right five people at a table and make the introduction feel natural.

Ask who trusts them, which rooms they bridge, what they contribute besides a title, what would make them recommend you, and what promise must never be broken after an introduction.

### 4. Gathering places

Map repeated gathering categories:

- Coffee, breakfast, lunch, and Ocean Drive routines
- Private clubs and golf through legitimate relationships—not awkward infiltration
- Churches, schools, youth sports, neighborhood meetings, and volunteer days
- Riverside Theatre, Vero Beach Museum of Art, galleries, performances, and arts events
- Fundraisers, nonprofits, civic groups, and chamber events
- Building departments, supply houses, showrooms, real estate offices, and jobsites
- Marinas, boat ramps, beaches, parks, and outdoor clubs
- Downtown events, markets, and small-business gatherings

For each, note cadence, purpose, convener, openness, and what useful contribution can be made. Showing up is not belonging; repeated helpful behavior closes the gap.

### 5. Connections

Draw bridges, not just dots:

- Who introduces island homeowners to trusted builders?
- Which trades are repeatedly requested by designers or property managers?
- Which nonprofit, school, church, or arts relationships connect separate circles?
- Who connects longtime families and newcomers?
- Which completed job can produce a permission-cleared neighbor introduction?
- Where can two people be introduced without asking either for anything?

Record relationship source, last meaningful contact, shared interests, promised follow-up, privacy limits, and next useful action. This must never become a creepy dossier. If a CRM note would be embarrassing when read aloud to the person, do not store it.

## Practical relationship-building playbook

### Before a conversation

1. Know the person's role, connection source, and one legitimate local question.
2. Choose one relevant proof point: a real project, process, credential, or referral.
3. Leave room for surprise. Research should prevent stupid questions, not eliminate curiosity.

### Open with observation, then invite a story

- “What brought you to Vero—weather, work, family, or a strategic retreat from humanity?”
- “How long have you been here, and what has development improved or ruined?”
- “Vero feels like one small town containing 47 social circles. Which ones actually get things done?”
- “What makes a contractor easy to recommend around here?”
- “Who is doing useful community work without making a parade out of it?”

Use the playful version only when the other person's tone invites it. The objective is a story, not a tight five minutes at an open mic.

### Listen for social currency

Listen for privacy, craftsmanship, operational competence, price clarity, community service, history, family convenience, technical evidence, or referral accountability. Match the value with proof. Do not pitch a content machine to a privacy-minded owner or deliver an origin-story sermon to an operator asking whether the crew can start Tuesday.

### Follow up like a local, not a funnel

Within one business day:

1. Reference the specific story or concern.
2. Deliver one promised answer, introduction, photo, checklist, or meeting time.
3. Offer one clear next step.
4. Record and complete the promise.
5. Never add the person to marketing without consent.

### Build relationship loops

```text
good work
→ documented proof with permission
→ neighbor or professional introduction
→ useful follow-up
→ second good experience
→ stronger reputation
```

Use the map for:

- **Sales:** personalize discovery questions and evidence.
- **Referrals:** make careful introductions among architects, designers, trades, property managers, and community connectors.
- **CRM:** capture relationship source, context, promised follow-up, and permission—not gossip.
- **Content:** turn real local questions into useful posts; keep spicy caricatures internal unless approved.
- **Neighborhood campaigns:** use job proof without exposing addresses or treating neighbors like coordinates.
- **Community presence:** contribute repeatedly without attaching an immediate sales demand.

## The persona

The intended voice is the **well-connected, slightly mischievous local observer**: able to sit at a charity luncheon, talk scheduling with a superintendent afterward, and make both groups laugh without making either feel like material.

- **Know the room:** its purpose, status cues, and privacy expectations.
- **Earn the joke:** familiarity before sharpness.
- **Bring value:** a useful answer, introduction, or completed promise.

## Four-week validation sprint

### Week 1 — geography and language

- Five conversations across island, downtown, and mainland settings
- Ask what locals call their area, where they gather, and what outsiders misunderstand
- Correct this guide with repeated language

### Week 2 — groups and influence

- Identify ten cross-group connectors and why people trust them
- Ask three professionals what makes a builder easy—or dangerous—to recommend
- Delete assumptions supported only by swagger

### Week 3 — places and connections

- Attend two open community gatherings without pitching
- Make two useful introductions and complete two promised follow-ups
- Map one path from an existing relationship to a legitimate new conversation

### Week 4 — business use

- Test three conversation openers
- Personalize five follow-ups from the map
- Ask: “Did that sound local and useful, or like a marketing department discovered linen?”
- Record introductions, appointments, and completed promises—not just contacts collected

## Scorecard

| Signal | Weekly measure |
|---|---|
| Local learning | Assumptions corrected; useful phrases captured |
| Presence | Recurring gatherings attended; contributions made |
| Reliability | Promised follow-ups completed on time |
| Connection | Useful introductions made and closed-loop |
| Trust | Unprompted referrals and repeat invitations |
| Business | Qualified conversations, estimates, and won work traceable to relationships |
| Restraint | Privacy respected; zero unauthorized stories or images |

The north-star measure is **trusted local relationships that produce better work and better introductions**. Contact count, club access, event attendance, and clever jokes are vanity metrics wearing boat shoes.

## Contributing

Treat every caricature as a hypothesis. Open an issue with the place, the irreverent one-liner, the useful social truth underneath it, evidence from real conversations, and what changed after testing it.

Do not submit private addresses, gossip, protected-trait stereotypes, or identifiable customer information.
